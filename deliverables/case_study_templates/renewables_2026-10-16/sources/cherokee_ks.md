## Key Takeaways

- **A county that pauses first**: a 2021 wind moratorium; 2022 wind rules with a 5,250-foot property-line setback; a commercial solar moratorium from June 2024 to July 2026; and a broader moratorium, adopted in December 2025 and renewed through March 31, 2027, on building commercial wind and solar and on using county roads and infrastructure for renewables, storage and data centers.
- **A stalled flagship project**: Clearway Energy's 225 MW Rainbow Springs Solar has leases with about a dozen landowners and a tax offer on the table, and Clearway has presented to the board "probably ten times." A matching request on the regional grid operator's queue holds an executed interconnection agreement for 2028. The project cannot be built while the moratorium stands.
- **A record well ahead of its profile:** Hawthorn Intelligence places Cherokee in only the 43^rd^ percentile (*i.e.,* 57% of U.S. counties score higher) for resemblance to counties with severe renewable restrictions. - **What to watch**: the county's first zoning code, now being drafted; the moratorium's March 31, 2027 end date; the November 3 race for the District 1 seat, which Commissioner Carlisle-Frazier is leaving; and the 2027 Kansas session.

## Summary

Cherokee County is closed to utility-scale wind and solar for now and is writing the rules that will decide whether it reopens. The three-member Board of County Commissioners adopted a wind moratorium in 2021 after RWE scouted a project, and a commercial solar moratorium that ran from June 2024 to July 2026 while Clearway sought its project. Since December 2025 a broader moratorium, now renewed through March 31, 2027, bars building commercial wind and solar and using county roads and infrastructure for renewables, storage, data centers and crypto mining. The county has no zoning today. A consultant is drafting its first zoning code under a comprehensive plan that lists wind farms, solar farms, battery storage and data centers as "high impact" uses.

Hawthorn Intelligence assigns Cherokee a County Restriction Score of 0.14, below the national base rate of 0.24. That puts it in the fifth national decile (one tenth of U.S. counties, ranked by score), the 43^rd^ percentile nationally and 67^th^ of 105 Kansas counties. The score measures how closely a county's profile resembles counties that have enacted severe restrictions; it is not a forecast for any project. Here the record matters more than the profile. Cherokee's restrictions are Verified against the county's own resolutions, and among the 747 counties with a severe restriction on record, its profile scores in only the 15^th^ percentile.

Developers come for the Riverton power plant's 161 kV substation and lines, farm and former mining land in the far southeast corner of Kansas, and landowners willing to lease. Opponents have raised the Tri-State Mining District's legacy of lead, zinc and cadmium in the soil and the cost of eventual cleanup, and residents at 2025 planning workshops rated utility-scale solar lowest of nine topics. The board split 2-1 on the September 2024 extension, the December 2025 moratorium, its 2026 renewal and the comprehensive plan, with Commissioner Myra Carlisle-Frazier in the minority each time; she is not running again.

# A county that pauses first

Cherokee's pattern was set in August 2021, when RWE Renewables began scouting a wind project and contacted landowners before the county. The commission unanimously adopted a one-year wind moratorium; then-Chair Cory Moates said the county was prepared to fight, and commissioners from neighboring Labette and Neosho counties urged the step. In October 2022 the board replaced the pause with Resolution 9-2022, which requires industrial wind turbines to stand 5,250 feet from the nearest property line, caps them at 440 feet and limits night noise to 39 dBA.

Solar followed the same path. On June 10, 2024, the board unanimously adopted Resolution 16-2024, a solar moratorium. It re-enacted the pause unanimously in August after a public meeting (Resolution 21-2024) and on September 9, 2024 extended it a year by Resolution 23-2024, 2-1, with Commissioner Myra Carlisle-Frazier opposed. The county counselor recommended zoning the whole county "for liability and transparency"; Carlisle-Frazier said she was "totally against zoning the county." In July 2025 the board unanimously extended the moratorium to July 1, 2026, at the planning commission's request, on Carlisle-Frazier's motion (Resolution 20-2025). It expired on that date; no 2026 resolution renews it.

On December 22, 2025 the board added a broader instrument, Resolution R-27-2025: a nine-month moratorium on building, or using county roads, resources or infrastructure for, commercial renewable energy projects, cryptocurrency mining, data centers, battery storage and other heavy industrial uses. It passed 2-1, with Carlisle-Frazier, then chair, opposed. On June 8, 2026, after the planning commission recommended a six-month extension, the board re-adopted it as Resolution 11-2026, running to March 31, 2027, again 2-1 with Carlisle-Frazier opposed.

Apart from a two-day lapse in August 2024, commercial solar in the county has been under a moratorium since June 2024, while the county drafts its first zoning code.

## Rainbow Springs Solar

Clearway Energy's **Rainbow Springs Solar** would place 225 MW of solar on private land southeast of Columbus, under leases signed by "a little more than a dozen" landowners and covering more than 3,000 acres. Clearway projects about 300 construction jobs, six to eight permanent jobs and about $42 million in local taxes. In July 2025 it [offered](https://digital-release.fourstateshomepage.com/?p=2309370) a contribution agreement equal to the taxes it would owe in years 11 to 30, "north of about $50 million," to cover the first decade of Kansas's renewable property-tax exemption. Wichita State University presented an economic impact study to the commission the same month.

The grid side is nearly complete. The Southwest Power Pool's queue lists a 225 MW solar-and-storage request on the Riverton to Neosho 161 kV line, GEN-2020-079, with an interconnection agreement fully executed and a planned operation date of August 15, 2028. Its size and location match Rainbow Springs, though no public document links the two by number. The county side is not complete. In September 2025 local officials [said](https://digital-release.fourstateshomepage.com/?p=2368043) they wanted to prevent the project, and Clearway's Barry Matchett said the company had "presented to the board probably ten times over the last 18 months." After the December 2025 moratorium, Clearway said it was "disheartened," that the decision "stifles economic development" and encroaches on property rights, and that it would keep working with the county and its landowners.

## Other projects

Three other solar and storage requests in the county have left the queue since 2023: a 200 MW hybrid at the Baxter Springs 161 kV substation (withdrawn December 2023), a 74 MW battery at Hockersville 138 kV (March 2024) and a 225 MW solar request on the Riverton to Neosho line (August 2025). RWE's 2021 wind effort never reached a filing. No data-center or crypto-mining proposal in the county has been reported, although R-27-2025 names both.

## Organized Opposition

Opposition in Cherokee comes from landowners, the planning commission and the commission majority rather than from a named group; we found no petition or organized group. At an August 2024 hearing, landowner Brett Warstler, whose land Clearway had sought, asked that $684 million be held in escrow for site restoration, and Matt Naiper warned that "cadmium, zinc, and lead" from the county's mining legacy could leach into groundwater. Another landowner, Dr. Brian Ipsen, supported the project and planned to graze livestock under the panels. Warstler now chairs the county planning commission that is drafting the zoning code.

At three comprehensive-plan workshops in October 2025, residents rated nine topics out of 10; utility-scale solar scored lowest (3.4) and utility-scale wind third-lowest (4.3). The [draft plan](https://engage.thinkconfluence.com/download_file/3051/832) records that "many community members supported prohibiting these uses entirely or implementing strict regulations," with a one-mile setback the typical suggestion.

## What to watch next

- The first county zoning code, being drafted by Confluence for the planning commission (next meeting November 4, 2026), and the setbacks it sets for wind, solar and storage.
- November 3, 2026: the District 1 commission seat, between Republican Scott Boyes and Democrat John J. Beitzinger, which Commissioner Carlisle-Frazier is leaving; also the Kansas governor's race.
- January 2027: the Kansas legislative session and any revival of SB 417-style state siting.
- March 31, 2027: scheduled end of Resolution 11-2026, and any further extension.
- August 15, 2028: Rainbow Springs' planned operation date under its interconnection agreement, and whether Clearway seeks a revised agreement with the county.

## How to Read Hawthorn Intelligence Reports

**County Restriction Score and decile:** A calibrated 0 to 1 measure of how closely a county resembles counties that have enacted severe wind, solar or battery-storage restrictions (an explicit moratorium or ban, or terms likely to act as one, such as a wind setback of half a mile or more). It is reported with its likely range and national decile (Table 1). It describes county profiles, not any specific project, and its drivers are predictive associations, not causes.

**Evidence status:** How thoroughly a county's restriction record has been checked against the instruments themselves. It describes verification effort, not the likelihood of a restriction.

**Outcome tiers:** Project results, recorded by what happened. Only terminal dispositions count as decided; a vote that has not produced a final disposition stays pending.

## Why developers are looking at Cherokee

**Power access:** Liberty Utilities' (Empire District) Riverton plant sits in the county's southeast corner. Its coal units were retired and demolished, and its 2016 conversion of a combustion turbine to a combined-cycle unit (268 MW nameplate) was the first of its kind in Kansas. The plant's 161 kV substation and the Riverton to Neosho line offer a short path to the grid; Rainbow Springs and a second 225 MW request both queued there. The county sits in the Southwest Power Pool near the Joplin, Missouri load center, where a developer has proposed a [hyperscale data-center park](https://www.joplinglobe.com/news/minnesota-company-plans-data-center-park-for-eastern-jasper-county/article_6f4b47a5-fa91-4f35-91d7-5cbfc1c4b789.html) with large solar and battery components in eastern Jasper County.

**Land use** is where the friction is. About 115 of Cherokee's square miles lie in the Tri-State Mining District's [Superfund site](https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/ks/cherokee-county-superfund-site-cherokee-county-kansas-fact-sheet-july-2021), listed in 1983 for lead, zinc and cadmium. Opponents argue that building on that ground risks disturbing contamination. Clearway's leases cover more than 3,000 acres of private land.

## State Context

Kansas leaves wind and solar siting to counties. SB 417 (2025-2026), requested on behalf of the Kansas Farm Bureau and Kansas Livestock Association, would have given the Kansas Corporation Commission permitting authority over large energy facilities and set decommissioning requirements; it [died](https://kslegislature.gov/li/b2025_26/measures/sb417/), and the 2026 session [adjourned](https://sitingsolutions.com/news/state-of-siting-april-2026/) without passing any siting bill. A 2024 law bars eminent domain for solar, as an earlier one did for wind. Counties have filled the gap: University of Kansas research found new wind banned in about one-fifth of Kansas counties, and in 2025 and 2026 Franklin, Saline, Marion, Shawnee and others adopted moratoria on renewables or data centers, while Montgomery County rejected one.

Data centers have reached the governor's race. The 2025 law SB 98 created a 20-year sales-tax exemption for large data centers. In October 2026 Democrat Cindy Holscher called for pausing development until guardrails exist, while Republican Ty Masterson defended the law and said local officials should decide, the [Kansas Reflector reported](https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-10-12/debate-about-kansas-law-incentivizing-data-center-construction-charges-up-governors-race).

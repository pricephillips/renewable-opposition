## Key Takeaways

- **A battery market, not a solar or wind market**: 776 MW of grid batteries operate in the county, 28 of its 33 active interconnection requests are for storage, and every contested project on record here is a battery.
- **Friction is city by city**: Pasadena paused battery permits, adopted a storage code in April 2025, and in January 2026 moved most of its limits into the fire code, keeping a five-acre site cap but dropping a 500-foot residential setback. Tomball denied a project in 2023. Baytown approved two in 2025 with conditions: one after labor groups raised safety concerns, the other over residents' objections on a 4-3 vote.
- **Above the average U.S. county:** Hawthorn Intelligence places Harris in the 78^th^ percentile (*i.e.,* 78% of U.S. counties score lower) for resemblance to counties with severe renewable restrictions, and 18^th^ of 254 in Texas. Its population and its interconnection queue account for most of that.
- **What to watch**: the state's battery fire-safety rules under HB 3824, which also limit local rules; the 2027 legislative session; a new county judge in January; and about 1.3 GW of queued storage at Pasadena-area and Baytown sites.

## Summary

Harris County is open to battery storage, but its zoned suburbs no longer approve it as a matter of course. Most of the county, the City of Houston and the unincorporated areas, has no zoning. In the unincorporated areas the county fire marshal's annual permit is the main local requirement; Houston applies its own fire code. We found no recorded opposition to the large batteries built on industrial land there. The suburban cities that do zone (Pasadena, Tomball and Baytown) have each paused, restricted, denied or narrowly approved a battery project since 2023.

Hawthorn Intelligence assigns Harris a County Restriction Score of 0.36, about one and a half times the national base rate of 0.24. That puts it in the eighth national decile (one tenth of U.S. counties, ranked by score), the 78^th^ percentile nationally and 18^th^ of 254 Texas counties. The score measures how closely a county's profile resembles counties that have enacted severe restrictions; it is not a forecast for any project. Population and interconnection-queue megawatts drive most of it. The restriction record is Verified: we read Pasadena's votes in its council minutes, and the moratorium extension and the ordinance that replaced it in its agenda packets.

Developers come for power-plant sites with high-voltage connections and fast-rising demand: CenterPoint expects 8 GW of data-center load by 2029. The objections on record are about proximity. Every contested project was a battery near homes, and opponents cited fire and toxic plumes, not views or farmland. Projects near housing in the zoned suburbs have faced special use permits, plume modeling and close votes.

# A county where the suburbs set the rules

Harris County has no general zoning power, and the City of Houston has no zoning either. The county's tool is its fire code, which covers the unincorporated areas; Houston applies its own. The Harris County Fire Marshal's Office requires a [$300 annual operating permit](https://hcfchub.net/ova_dep/energy-storage-systems/) for energy-storage systems, and Fire Marshal Laurie Christensen told legislators the county code was amended for lithium-ion storage effective January 1 and October 1, 2025. The suburban cities that zone have gone through the sequence seen elsewhere in Texas: a pause, then rules, then contested permits.

Pasadena moved first. In October 2024 its council unanimously adopted a 90-day moratorium on commercial battery permits, citing "several inquiries from prospective developers" and battery-site fires nationwide. It extended the pause unanimously in January 2025 and, on April 1, 2025, replaced it with Article XII of its building code. As presented in the March 18 agenda packet, Article XII caps sites at five acres; requires 1,500 feet between battery sites, 500 feet from residential property and 1,000 feet from schools, hospitals and other critical facilities; requires a site within 150 feet of CenterPoint property; bars mapped flood zones; and requires a special use permit backed by a hazard mitigation analysis, a fire plan and plume modeling.

On October 21, 2025, the council unanimously adopted Ordinance 2025-205, which adopts the 2024 International Fire Code with local amendments and repeals Article XII, stating that "requirements regarding energy storage systems" were being updated in the fire code; the change took effect on January 19, 2026. The new Section 1208 keeps the five-acre cap (now excluding setback areas), 1,500 feet between battery sites, 1,000 feet from a shorter list of critical facilities that no longer names schools, 150-foot side and rear setbacks, the flood-zone bar, and a special use permit with CenterPoint review. It drops the 500-foot setback from residential property, the rule that a site lie within 150 feet of CenterPoint property, and the plume study.

Pasadena has regulated storage rather than banning it. Every council vote was unanimous, and the moratorium and Article XII hearings drew two speakers in total.

## Baytown's close votes

Baytown decides each battery project by special use permit, rezoning the land where needed. It approved its first, an uncontested battery on Garth Road, in 2023, when battery storage was an unlisted industrial use; its 2025 code lists battery storage as a use allowed with a special use permit. In August 2025 the council voted 7-0 to approve Cypress Creek Renewables' 100 MW **Massey** project on about 15 acres. Labor groups raised safety concerns: Laborers International Union Local 350, IBEW Local 527 and the Texas Climate Jobs Project asked the city in writing to postpone the permit, and speakers for the IBEW and the Texas Climate Jobs Project asked the council to delay its vote. Robert Ramos, speaking for the laborers' union, said that if required it would "initiate a ballot of signatures," according to the council minutes. The conditions in the draft ordinance require battery containers at least 200 feet from homes and 100 feet from other structures, a six-foot masonry wall and landscaped bufferyards.

In November 2025 Balanced Rock Power's **Claire** project, up to 400 MW (406 MW in ERCOT's queue) on about nine acres zoned Suburban Residential next to a CenterPoint substation, won a planning commission recommendation of approval, with Commissioners Mike Beard and Kim Kosteck voting no. The developer said it had increased the setbacks and added flood retention and a six-foot masonry wall. The council approved it 4-3, with conditions that put battery containers at least 200 feet from homes. Opponents at the council hearing cited the site's closeness to homes, fire and toxic gas risks, insurance exclusions for lithium-ion battery fires and questions about future ownership. Claire was approved in a residential district by one vote.

## Other projects

**Tomball** denied a conditional use permit in June 2023 for a Black Mountain Energy Storage battery on Pitchford Road. Of the property owners notified within 2,000 feet, 11 opposed and 6 supported, and the council voted unanimously against it after the mayor said the city was "not ready for this in our 13 square miles." Black Mountain now holds a 201 MW request at Greens Bayou.

In Houston and the unincorporated county, we found no recorded opposition to the large projects. Jupiter Power's **Callisto I** (about 200 MW) began operating in August 2024 at the former H.O. Clarke plant site, where Callisto II and Pamela Heights I are under construction. Cypress Creek's 200 MW **Destiny** and the 204 MW **Citadel** batteries operate near Crosby, and X-Elio's **Liberty** hybrid pairs 67 MW of solar with 65 MW of storage on the Liberty County line. AES has two requests of about 408 MW each, **Beacon** and **Sigma**, at the former Deepwater plant in the Pasadena area, still in study. The public record does not establish whether they fall inside Pasadena's limits and so under its five-acre cap.

## Organized Opposition

Opposition in Harris has been local, project-specific and largely unorganized. We found no named resident group, Facebook group or petition aimed at a Harris County project. The objections on record came from residents near the Claire site in Baytown, from property owners notified of the Tomball permit and, on Baytown's Massey project, from Laborers International Union Local 350, IBEW Local 527 and the Texas Climate Jobs Project.

The organized campaigns are next door. In League City (Galveston County), neighbors of an elementary school petitioned against a 200 MW Cypress Creek project in 2024, and the city paused storage and then adopted an ordinance. In Katy, across the line in Waller County, the city denied Vesper Energy's 500 MW Ochoa project near Katy High School in October 2024; the developer asked the state utility commission to override the city, ERCOT intervened, and Vesper withdrew in July 2026. Statewide, the Republican Party of Texas adopted a [resolution](https://texasgop.org/resolution-to-protect-texas-counties-from-the-rapid-expansion-of-battery-energy-storage-systems-bess/) in March 2025 calling for limits on battery storage.

## What to watch next

- Texas Department of Insurance: the formal proposal and adoption of HB 3824 fire-safety rules for batteries entering service on or after January 1, 2027, and how the law's bar on "inconsistent" local rules is read against codes like Pasadena's.
- November 3, 2026: county judge (Plummer v. Sanchez) and Commissioners Court Precincts 2 and 4; the new court sits in January 2027.
- January 2027: the 90th Legislature convenes, the first session since a state permit for renewable plants (SB 819) died in committee.
- Baytown: Claire and Massey, which ERCOT data list with projected commercial operation dates of December 2027 and January 2028.
- AES Beacon and Sigma at Deepwater (projected October 2029) and whether Pasadena's fire code siting limits apply to them.
- CenterPoint's data-center load additions and ERCOT's Batch Zero results.

## How to Read Hawthorn Intelligence Reports

**County Restriction Score and decile:** A calibrated 0 to 1 measure of how closely a county resembles counties that have enacted severe wind, solar or battery-storage restrictions (an explicit moratorium or ban, or terms likely to act as one, such as a solar or storage setback of 1,000 feet or more). It is reported with its likely range and national decile (Table 1). It describes county profiles, not any specific project, and its drivers are predictive associations, not causes.

**Evidence status:** How thoroughly a county's restriction record has been checked against the instruments themselves. It describes verification effort, not the likelihood of a restriction.

**Outcome tiers:** Project results, recorded by what happened. Only terminal dispositions count as decided; a vote that has not produced a final disposition stays pending.

## Why developers are looking at Harris

**Power access:** Power-plant sites offer ready-made interconnections: Jupiter's Callisto projects sit on the former H.O. Clarke site, AES's Beacon and Sigma at the former Deepwater station, and Black Mountain's Outer Loop at the Greens Bayou plant, which still runs gas turbines. Demand is rising: CenterPoint has 12.2 GW of committed industrial load, [expects](https://www.rigzone.com/news/centerpoint_targets_8_gw_of_data_center_load_in_3_years-26-apr-2026-183533-article/) 8 GW of data centers by 2029, and submitted more than 17 GW to ERCOT's ["Batch Zero"](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/centerpoint-energy-14-gw-texas-batch-zero-large-load-earnings/826329/) large-load study. After Hurricane Beryl cut power to about 2.26 million CenterPoint customers in July 2024, the Public Utility Commission [approved](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/texas-regulators-trim-approve-27b-centerpoint-system-resiliency-plan/758476/) a roughly $2.7 billion resiliency plan, and in September 2026 the county broke ground on an [$88 million community solar and storage program](https://communityimpact.com/bay-area/environment/harris-county-launches-affordable-clean-energy-program/).

**Land use** is where the friction is. We found no recorded opposition on industrial land such as H.O. Clarke. On suburban land, as with Claire's nine acres zoned Suburban Residential in Baytown, proximity to homes became the issue. Pasadena's 2025 code made the trade explicit: storage only near utility property and away from homes, schools and flood zones. Its 2026 fire code keeps the flood-zone bar and the distance from critical facilities but no longer names homes or schools.

**How the state law was written:** HB 3824, signed June 20, 2025, directs the Commissioner of Insurance to adopt fire standards "based solely on" UL and NFPA 855 for batteries of 1 MWh or more entering service on or after January 1, 2027, and [provides](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/HB03824F.htm) that, unless another statute expressly authorizes it, "a municipality or county may not adopt, enforce, or maintain an ordinance, order, or rule regulating conduct in a field of regulation that is inconsistent with the standards." Pasadena's fire code now carries both fire-design rules and land-use siting limits. Which parts survive depends on the department's final rules, still an [informal working draft](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/rules/index.html) on October 16, 2026.

## State Context

Texas has no state siting permit for wind, solar or storage plants, and the 2025 session left that unchanged. SB 819, which would have required one for renewable plants of 10 MW or more, passed the Senate and [died](https://capitol.texas.gov/BillLookup/History.aspx?LegSess=89R&Bill=SB819) in House State Affairs, as did SB 388 and SB 715. What passed was storage-specific: HB 3809 adds decommissioning and financial-assurance terms to battery leases signed from September 1, 2025, and HB 3824 creates state fire standards for batteries entering service from 2027 and limits local rules inconsistent with them. SB 6 changes how large loads such as data centers connect to the ERCOT grid.

The House State Affairs Committee held an interim hearing on battery safety on April 23, 2026, where most of the roughly 180 [public comments](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/handouts/C4502026042310001/63fa389c-78a1-45e9-9914-c39bb46ecf2c.PDF) came from small towns; the Senate Natural Resources Committee took up wind and solar decommissioning in September 2026.

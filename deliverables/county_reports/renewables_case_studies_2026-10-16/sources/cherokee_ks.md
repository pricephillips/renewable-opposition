## Key Takeaways

- **High-risk permitting environment**: a 2021 wind moratorium, 2022 wind rules with a 5,250-foot property-line setback, a commercial solar moratorium in force since June 2024, a moratorium the board has now extended to March 31, 2027, and a December 2025 moratorium on county roads and infrastructure for renewables, storage and data centers.
- **A blocked flagship project**: Clearway Energy's 225 MW Rainbow Springs Solar holds an executed grid interconnection agreement for 2028 operation, leases with about a dozen landowners and a tax offer the county has heard "probably ten times," yet it cannot proceed while the moratorium stands.
- **Record far ahead of profile:** Hawthorn Intelligence ranks Cherokee only in the 43^rd^ percentile (*e.g.,* 57% of U.S. counties are graded riskier) for resemblance to a county with severe renewable restrictions, yet it holds two severe restrictions and sits in the 95^th^ percentile nationally for recorded opposition per resident.
- **Near-term risks** hinge on the county's first zoning code, now being drafted after a 2-1 vote to adopt a comprehensive plan, the moratorium's March 31, 2027 end date, the November 3 commission election, and the 2027 Kansas session.

## Summary

Cherokee County, KS is effectively closed to utility-scale wind and solar for now and is writing the rules that will decide whether it reopens. The three-member Board of County Commissioners has adopted a moratorium every time a large renewable project has approached: on wind in 2021, on commercial solar in June 2024 (renewed each year since), and on the use of county roads and infrastructure for renewables, storage, data centers and crypto mining in December 2025. It has no zoning today; a consultant is drafting a first zoning code under a comprehensive plan that lists wind farms, solar farms, battery storage and data centers as "high impact" uses.

Hawthorn Intelligence assigns Cherokee a County Restriction Score of 0.14, below the national base rate of 0.24, placing it in the fifth national decile (one tenth of U.S. counties, ranked by score) and the 43^rd^ percentile among U.S. counties. Within Kansas it ranks 67^th^ of 105 counties; Harvey (0.72), Douglas (0.67) and Shawnee (0.67) score highest, and the state median is 0.18. The score is a calibrated measure of resemblance to counties that have enacted severe restrictions, not a project-specific forecast. Its restriction record is Verified against the county's own resolutions, and it is that record, not the profile, that should drive the read: among the 747 counties with a severe restriction on record, Cherokee's profile scores in only the 15^th^ percentile.

Contextually, Cherokee illustrates how a small, rural county with no zoning can stop a well-advanced project with a sequence of short moratoria. Clearway's Rainbow Springs Solar, 225 MW southeast of Columbus, entered the regional grid operator's queue in 2020 and now holds a fully executed interconnection agreement with a planned operation date of August 15, 2028. Clearway offered contribution payments "north of about $50 million" to cover the 10-year state property-tax exemption, and commissioned an economic impact study. The commission extended its solar moratorium anyway, and in September 2025 local officials told reporters they wanted to prevent the project.

On the surface, the county offers developers clear benefits: the Riverton power plant's 161 kV substation and lines, flat farm and former mining land in the far southeast corner of Kansas, and landowners willing to lease. Nevertheless, land-use conflict has proven decisive. Opponents have raised the Tri-State Mining District's legacy of lead, zinc and cadmium in the soil, the cost of eventual removal, farmland loss and property rights, and the county's planning survey found utility-scale solar the least popular of every land use it asked about.

For developers, investors, and policymakers, Cherokee is a high-friction jurisdiction whose rules are still being written. The board has divided 2-1 on every step since 2024, with Chair Myra Carlisle-Frazier opposing moratoria and zoning, and she was not on this year's primary ballot. Near-term risk turns on the zoning code and any setbacks it sets, the moratorium's March 31, 2027 expiry, the District 1 election, and statewide siting bills expected in 2027.

# A county that pauses first

Cherokee's pattern was set in August 2021, when RWE Renewables began scouting a wind project and contacted landowners before the county. The commission unanimously adopted a one-year wind moratorium; then-Chair Cory Moates said the county was prepared to fight, and commissioners from neighboring Labette and Neosho counties urged the step. In October 2022 the board replaced the pause with Resolution 9-2022, which requires industrial wind turbines to stand 5,250 feet from the nearest property line, caps them at 440 feet and limits night noise to 39 dBA, terms that leave little room for a wind farm.

Solar followed the same path. On June 10, 2024, as Clearway's Rainbow Springs project came forward, the board adopted Resolution 16-2024, a solar moratorium; it re-enacted the pause in August after a public meeting, and on September 9, 2024 extended it a year by Resolution 23-2024, 2-1, with Commissioner Myra Carlisle-Frazier opposed. The county counselor recommended zoning the whole county "for liability and transparency"; Carlisle-Frazier said she was "totally against zoning the county." In July 2025 the board extended the solar moratorium to July 1, 2026 at the planning commission's request (Resolution 20-2025), and in 2026, according to the planning commission's [minutes](https://cherokeecountyks.gov/main/images/pdfs/CCPC/CCPC_Minutes_7126.pdf), it voted "to extend the existing moratorium to March 31, 2027," most likely this solar moratorium.

On December 22, 2025 the board added a broader instrument, Resolution R-27-2025: a nine-month moratorium on approving requests to use county roads, resources or infrastructure for commercial renewable energy, cryptocurrency mining, data centers, battery storage and other heavy industrial uses. It passed 2-1, with Carlisle-Frazier, by then chair, opposed. We found no record of whether it was extended past its scheduled end in late September 2026.

This sequence matters because it shows that Cherokee's majority has chosen to stop projects while it writes rules, not after. The county has not banned solar outright, but it has kept a solar moratorium in force for more than two years and is now drafting the zoning that will replace it.

## Rainbow Springs Solar

**Rainbow Springs Solar** is the county's test case. Clearway Energy's project would place 225 MW of solar on privately owned land southeast of Columbus, under leases signed by "a little more than a dozen" landowners and covering more than 3,000 acres. Clearway projects about 300 construction jobs, six to eight permanent jobs, and about $42 million in local taxes; in July 2025 it [offered](https://digital-release.fourstateshomepage.com/?p=2309370) a contribution agreement equal to the taxes it would owe in years 11 to 30, "north of about $50 million," to cover the first decade of Kansas's renewable property-tax exemption. Wichita State University presented an economic impact study to the commission the same month.

The grid side is nearly complete. The Southwest Power Pool's queue lists a 225 MW solar-and-storage request on the Riverton to Neosho 161 kV line, GEN-2020-079, with an interconnection agreement fully executed and a planned operation date of August 15, 2028; its size and location match Rainbow Springs, though no public document links the two by number. The county side is not. In September 2025 local officials [said](https://digital-release.fourstateshomepage.com/?p=2368043) they wanted to prevent the project, and Clearway's Barry Matchett said the company had "presented to the board probably ten times over the last 18 months." After the December 2025 moratorium, Clearway said it was "disheartened," that the decision "stifles economic development" and encroaches on property rights, and that it would keep working with the county and its landowners.

For future projects, Rainbow Springs demonstrates that in a county with no zoning, a signed interconnection agreement and a generous tax offer are not enough. The project's fate now depends on what the new zoning code allows and when the moratorium ends.

## Other projects

Three other solar and storage requests in the county have left the queue since 2023: a 200 MW hybrid at the Baxter Springs 161 kV substation (withdrawn December 2023), a 74 MW battery at Hockersville 138 kV (March 2024), and a 225 MW solar request on the Riverton to Neosho line (August 2025). RWE's 2021 wind effort never reached a filing. No data-center or crypto-mining proposal in the county has been reported, although R-27-2025 names both.

## Organized Opposition

Opposition in Cherokee comes from landowners, the planning commission and the commission majority rather than from a named group. We found no petition or organized group. At an August 2024 hearing, landowner Brett Warstler, whose land Clearway had sought, asked that $684 million be held in escrow for site restoration, and resident Matt Naiper warned that "cadmium, zinc, and lead" from the county's mining legacy could leach into groundwater. Another landowner, Dr. Brian Ipsen, supported the project and planned to graze livestock under the panels. Warstler now chairs the county planning commission that is drafting the zoning code.

The county's 2026 comprehensive-plan survey put the sentiment in numbers. Asked to rate land uses out of 10, residents gave utility-scale solar 3.4 and utility-scale wind 4.3, the lowest of any category; the [draft plan](https://engage.thinkconfluence.com/download_file/3051/832) records that "many community members supported prohibiting these uses entirely or implementing strict regulations," with a one-mile setback the typical suggestion.

That political environment increases uncertainty for applicants. Even a project that pays more than its taxes and holds a grid agreement faces a board majority and a planning commission that have repeatedly chosen to pause.

## What to watch next

Several developments will shape Cherokee's near-term outlook:

- The first county zoning code, being drafted by Confluence for the planning commission, and the setbacks it sets for wind, solar and storage.
- The extended moratorium's scheduled end on March 31, 2027, and any further extension.
- Whether R-27-2025, the roads and infrastructure moratorium, was or will be extended.
- The November 3 election for the District 1 seat, where Republican nominee Scott Boyes, a member of the planning commission, is on the ballot and Chair Carlisle-Frazier was not on the primary ballot.
- Rainbow Springs' August 2028 target, and whether Clearway seeks a revised agreement with the county.

Cherokee's experience shows how quickly a rural market with willing landowners and a strong substation can become closed. The county's grid access and land remain real advantages. But applicants should assume that local acceptance will depend on more than tax payments. Projects will need to address mining contamination, farmland, decommissioning, visual impact, and a set of setbacks that residents want measured in miles.

## How to Read Hawthorn Intelligence Reports

**County Restriction Score and decile:** A calibrated 0 to 1 measure of how closely a county resembles counties that have enacted severe wind, solar or battery-storage restrictions (an explicit moratorium or ban, or terms likely to act as one, such as a wind setback of half a mile or more), reported with its likely range and its national decile (Table 1). It is a resemblance measure over county profiles, not a forecast for any specific project, and its drivers are predictive associations, not causes.

**Evidence status:** How thoroughly a county's restriction record has been checked against the instruments themselves. It describes verification effort, not the likelihood of a restriction.

## Why developers are looking at Cherokee

**Power access:** The Riverton power plant in the county's southeast corner anchors Liberty Utilities' (Empire District) transmission in the region. Its coal units were retired and demolished, and its 2016 conversion of a combustion turbine to a 268 MW combined-cycle unit was the first of its kind in Kansas. The plant's 161 kV substation and the Riverton to Neosho line give solar projects a short path to the grid, which is why Rainbow Springs and a second 225 MW request queued there. The county sits in the Southwest Power Pool, near the Joplin, Missouri load center, where a developer has proposed a [hyperscale data-center park](https://www.joplinglobe.com/news/minnesota-company-plans-data-center-park-for-eastern-jasper-county/article_6f4b47a5-fa91-4f35-91d7-5cbfc1c4b789.html) with large solar and battery components in eastern Jasper County.

**Land use** tends to be the friction point. Much of Cherokee lies within the Tri-State Mining District, where the federal government has overseen cleanup of lead, zinc and cadmium for decades; opponents argue that building on that ground risks disturbing contamination, while the same history leaves former mined land that is hard to farm. Leases cover thousands of acres of private farmland, and the county's planning survey shows residents rank farmland preservation and rural character well above energy development.

## State Context

Kansas leaves wind and solar siting to counties, and lawmakers have not changed that. SB 417 (2025-2026), requested on behalf of the Kansas Farm Bureau and Kansas Livestock Association, would have given the Kansas Corporation Commission permitting authority over large energy facilities and set decommissioning requirements; it [died](https://kslegislature.gov/li/b2025_26/measures/sb417/), and the 2026 session [adjourned](https://sitingsolutions.com/news/state-of-siting-april-2026/) without passing any siting bill. A 2024 law already bars eminent domain for solar, as it did for wind. Counties have filled the gap: research from the University of Kansas found new wind banned in about one-fifth of Kansas counties, and in 2025 and 2026 Franklin, Saline, Marion, Shawnee and others adopted moratoria on renewables or data centers, while Montgomery County rejected one.

Data centers have reached the governor's race. The 2025 law SB 98 created a 20-year sales-tax exemption for large data centers; in October 2026 Democrat Cindy Holscher called for pausing development until guardrails exist, while Republican Ty Masterson defended the law and said local officials should decide, the [Kansas Reflector reported](https://www.hppr.org/hppr-news/2026-10-12/debate-about-kansas-law-incentivizing-data-center-construction-charges-up-governors-race).
